\section{Experiments II: rules against a ring that adapts}\label{sec:policy}

\subsection{Design}

\paragraph{Levers.} The daily price band ($7\%$ today, $10\%$, $15\%$ or none); the reference price of off-book block trades (the last price, or the mean over the last $L=60$ or $240$ steps); inspection of the $B$ top-ranked accounts per window by the signed test, with a fine of $m$ times the illegal gain; a minimum resting time before an order may be cancelled (5 or 15 steps); a fee per cancelled order; a larger tick; a circuit breaker. With $m=5$ and $m=10$, deterrence needs a probability of detection above $1/m$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:deter}).

\paragraph{Manipulators.} The ring chooses a strategy from the 11-parameter space of Table~\ref{tab:space}. Because single evolution-strategy runs were noisy and non-monotone across policies, every policy faces the same set of four ring strategies (the strongest found under different policies in one market, the default ring, and the strongest unconstrained one); the ring picks the best on 12 selection episodes and the tables report 12 separate episodes (Proposition~\ref{prop:lower}: a lower bound on a fully adaptive ring). The one unrestricted evolution-strategy search we ran, in a single market, gave non-monotone gains across policies (101k with no band, 49k with a 7\% band, 94k with 10\%, 84k with 15\%; Appendix~\ref{app:single}), so the noise of such a search is of the order of the effects we interpret, which is why the menu is cross-evaluated; we have not shown that the menu is near the optimum for each policy, and every conclusion below is conditional on it (Section~\ref{sec:menu} adds 24 random strategies to the menu in all 16 markets). The spoofer, the pump-and-dump agent and the wash pair use fixed strategies and are evaluated for what they achieve; for them the effect of a rule is an upper bound.

\paragraph{Families of markets.} Market parameters (numbers of noise and fundamental traders, market-maker activity and sensitivity to displayed depth, volatility of the latent value, momentum activity) are drawn at random, honest-only markets are simulated, and a draw is kept if its cancel share (0.27--0.37), retail share (0.66--0.85) and daily volatility (0.9--1.4\% for the index-level family, 1.8--2.8\% for the stock-level family) are within tolerance of the real ones, an approximate Bayesian rejection. We keep six markets per family (6 of 27 draws and 6 of 62 draws) and report the distribution of each outcome over markets. Acceptance used only two episodes per draw, so it is noisy: re-measured over 16 episodes (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}), the volatility is inside the tolerance in five of six index-level and four of six stock-level markets, the cancel share in five of six in each family and the retail share in all, and the excursions are of up to about two standard errors of the re-measurement for the volatility and of at most 0.008 for the cancel share. Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} compares the first accepted markets with real returns.

\subsection{The ring against each rule}

Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble} and Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} give the ring's gain in the index-level family. The daily price band does not change the ring's gain (median ratio to no policy $0.97$; the ring moves the price by about 0.5\%, far inside a band of 7\%), and widening it to 10\% or 15\%, which the securities commission is studying, changes nothing in the single market where we tried it (Appendix~\ref{app:single}). Pricing blocks at the trailing mean cuts the gain, Section~\ref{sec:blockcheck} analyses how much. Inspection deters the ring only with enough capacity: with three inspected accounts per window (0.5\% of accounts) the ring's expected net benefit at $m=5$ is non-positive in five of six markets, with one inspected account in two of six; the median probability of being caught is 42\% and 17\%.

\paragraph{Stock-level family.} The case stocks are more volatile than the index, so we repeat the exercise with six markets whose daily volatility is 1.8--2.8\% (Table~\ref{tab:ensemblestock}; same ring strategy set, not re-optimised). The ring's gain with no policy is larger (median 164k against 110k at index level). The conclusions on the band and on inspection are unchanged in sign: the band leaves the gain at a median 99\% of its value. Under inspection the best-responding ring shrinks its operation: its median gain falls to 3\% of the no-policy gain (6k), with a median probability of being caught of 8\% with one inspected account per window and 25\% with three. The ring's expected net benefit at $m=5$ is non-positive in three of six markets with one inspected account and in five of six with three; in the three markets where one inspected account does not deter, the ring still nets up to 41k, and in the one market where three do not, 7k. The detection probabilities are lower than at index level, yet deterrence is not worse, because the ring's remaining gain is smaller relative to the fine; we have not separated the two effects. In both families the price band has no effect, block pricing and inspection have large ones, and no single rule removes the ring's gain in every market.

\subsection{Markets without trending}\label{sec:acf}

The simulated daily returns of the families above trend (lag-1 autocorrelation 0.4 to 0.6, Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}c), whereas the daily returns of VN30 and of HOSE stocks have no serial correlation. The trending comes from the slow adjustment of the price to the latent value, and a ring may profit from it. To check whether the conclusions depend on it, we drew a third family with many more fundamental traders (80 to 220 against 25 to 60, so that the price follows the latent value within a day), more noise traders and market makers, and accepted a draw only if, besides the volatility (0.9--1.5\%) and the cancel share, the lag-1 autocorrelation of daily returns of four honest episodes of 6000 steps was at most 0.2 in absolute value; the retail-share tolerance was widened to 0.55--0.85 because more fundamental traders lower it. Four of 29 draws were accepted (autocorrelation 0.03 to 0.20, volatility 1.05--1.37\%, retail share 0.62--0.65), so this family has four markets, and its moments were not re-measured.

Table~\ref{tab:ensembleacf} gives the results. The band again has no effect (median ratio 1.06). In three of the four markets the ring is profitable without a rule (35k to 64k); in the fourth it loses money (\textminus40k) whatever the rule. Pricing blocks at the trailing mean removes the gain in two of the three profitable markets (to \textminus0.5k and \textminus1.1k at 240 steps) and in the third leaves 55\% of it (28k against 52k), where the ring's gain comes mostly from trading. Inspection is less effective than in the other families: the median probability of being caught is 4\% with one and with three inspected accounts, and the ring's expected net benefit at $m=5$ is non-positive in two of four markets, one of which is the market where the ring loses money anyway; in the market where it is not caught at all it nets 65k. Markets with more accounts per window (about 630 to 1070 noise and fundamental traders against 400 to 670 before) are the markets where the power law predicts weaker detection (Section~\ref{sec:powercheck}). The conclusion on the band and on the block price therefore survives the removal of the trending; the conclusion on inspection depends on the number of accounts, and the capacity needed grows with it.

\subsection{Is the four-strategy menu enough?}\label{sec:menu}

The review of any best-response result starts with the strategy set. In each of the 16 accepted markets we gave the ring, for three policies (the band alone, the band with block prices at the 240-step mean, and the band with inspection of three accounts per window), a menu of 28 strategies: the four of the ensembles and 24 drawn uniformly in the 11-parameter space of Table~\ref{tab:space}. Selection has two stages on separate episodes (all 28 on six episodes; the four menu strategies and the five best on twelve more), and the chosen strategy is scored on twelve fresh episodes, so that the maximum over many noisy estimates does not enter the reported gain; a single stage with six episodes picked noise in a trial run (a reported gain of 23k against 93k for the menu of four in one cell). Table~\ref{tab:menurandom} compares the ring with the menu of four with the ring with the larger menu.

Against the band alone and against block pricing the larger menu changes nothing: in 15 of 16 markets the strategy chosen from 28 is one of the four, and the medians are identical. The share of the gain that survives block pricing at 240 steps is 0.32 in both cases, and in every market the gain falls. Against inspection the larger menu chooses a random strategy in 13 of 16 markets, but its gain on the fresh episodes is not higher than the menu's (median difference $-10$k over these 13 cells, higher in 5), and the number of markets in which inspection of three accounts leaves the ring a positive net benefit at $m=5$ goes from 3 to 4 of 16, the extra market being in the trend-free family. We read this as no evidence that the four-strategy menu understates the ring's best response by much in the three policies, with two caveats: 24 random points in 11 dimensions are a weak search, and an adaptive search against inspection would exploit the structure of the detector better than random draws do. The deterrence conclusions therefore stay upper bounds for the regulator (Proposition~\ref{prop:lower}), by a margin that this check does not bound from above.

\paragraph{Intervals over markets.} The unit of replication of the ensemble results is the market, and with six markets per family the intervals are wide. Over the 16 markets of the ensembles, the ring's gain with block prices at the 240-step mean falls in all 15 markets where the band-only gain is positive (exact sign test $p<0.001$); the median ratio to the band-only gain is 0.12 (bootstrap 95\% interval over markets $-0.01$ to 0.24; index level 0.11, stock level 0.20, trend-free $-0.01$), and at 60 steps 0.36 (0.13 to 0.55). Inspection of three accounts per window leaves the ring a positive net benefit at $m=5$ in 4 of 16 markets (Wilson 95\% interval 10 to 49\%), with a median probability of detection of 21\%. The fresh episodes of this section give a larger median ratio for block pricing (0.32), because the ratio of two noisy gains with a small denominator is itself noisy, but not a different sign. We therefore state the direction of the block-price and band results with confidence and their size as a range.

\subsection{The block reference price: law, ex-post evaluation and best response}\label{sec:blockcheck}

Because the reference price enters only the ring's payoff (Section~\ref{sec:blocklaw}), the effect of the rule for every $L$ can be computed exactly from the same simulated episodes. For each market of both families and each of the four strategies we simulate 12 selection and 12 reporting episodes once and score them with $L\in\{1,5,15,30,60,120,240,480\}$. Table~\ref{tab:block} and Figure~\ref{fig:block} report the median over markets. The \emph{block term} is the value of the block alone (block size times credited displacement); the total gain adds trading profit, which the rule does not touch. The rule removes most of the block term: a reference of 60 steps leaves 42\% (index level) and 39\% (stock level) of it, a one-day reference 13\% and 2\%. The linear-ramp law of Proposition~\ref{prop:block}, with the measured push duration $a=72$ to $83$ steps, has the right shape and order of magnitude but overstates the retained value at $L\ge30$ (0.61 against 0.42 at $L=60$ at index level, 0.16 against 0.13 at $L=240$), because the real price path is not a ramp ending at the sale. The total gain falls to a median 52\% (60 steps) and 28\% (240 steps) of its value at index level and to 68\% and 44\% at stock level, as the trading profit remains; with a reference of two days ($L=480$) the median total gain is about zero ($-0.03$ at index level, $0.12$ at stock level), and in markets where the unconstrained gain is small the ring loses money already at 60 to 240 steps. Re-selecting the strategy (``adaptive'') changes the median total gain by 0.13 or less in either direction: within the four strategies the ring finds no good counter, and the gap between the adaptive and the static curve is the value of adaptation. The independent episodes of the earlier ensemble run (Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}) give a lower retained share of the total gain (35\% and 11\% at index level, 45\% and 20\% at stock level for 60 and 240 steps); with episode-to-episode standard errors of half the mean in gain or more, we read the evidence as: a one-day reference leaves the ring between roughly one tenth and one half of its gain, and removes most of the block value.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the block size.} The block of 3000 lots is an assumption of the simulator, and it matters: the rule acts on the block term only, so its effect on the total gain depends on how much of the gain comes from the block. The size enters only the payoff, so a different size can be evaluated exactly on the same episodes (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). With a block of 10,000 lots the ring keeps 19--21\% of its gain at a one-day reference; with the assumed size 28--44\%; with a block of 1000 lots 60--64\%, and of 300 lots 83--87\%, because trading profit then dominates. The rule is effective against a ring whose gain is realised through off-book blocks and has no effect on a ring that profits through trading on the book.

\subsection{Inspection capacity and the deterrence frontier}\label{sec:frontier}

Proposition~\ref{prop:deter} turns the power law into a capacity requirement. With the constants fitted in Section~\ref{sec:powercheck} ($s_0=1.20$), Table~\ref{tab:capacity} gives the number of accounts that must be inspected per window to deter a ring of three accounts active in four windows, and the saving over random inspection. In a market with 600 active accounts per window, screening deters at a fine of five times the gain with about 3 inspected accounts per window (0.5\% of accounts) against 11 for random inspection, and at ten times with 1.2 against 5.2; doubling the fine multiple therefore saves a factor of about 2.4 in capacity, which gives the regulator two exchangeable instruments (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). The advantage of screening shrinks as the market grows (saving of 3.7 at 600 accounts and 2.0 at 2000). The model's detection probability for a ring of three accounts in four windows with one and three inspected accounts (8\% and 20\%) is close to the probability found for the best-responding ring in the stock-level family (8\% and 25\%) and below that of the index-level family (17\% and 42\%; Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}a). We do not know which sizes the best-responding ring chose, so this agreement is suggestive, not a test.

\subsection{Where to inspect: allocating capacity over stocks}\label{sec:allocation}

The deterrence frontier above is for one stock. A regulator spreads one capacity over many stocks that differ in size, and Proposition~\ref{prop:alloc} gives the rule that equalises the ring's net benefit. We test it in the Gaussian campaign model of Proposition~\ref{prop:power}, where the signal-to-noise ratio of a member ($\eta=0.25$) and the ring ($K=8$) are the same in every stock and only the market differs: eight stocks of 100 to 2,500 accounts, a prize that grows with the stock (200$N_w^{0.7}$ with a lognormal factor of standard deviation 0.5, independent of detection), 400 ring episodes per stock, half used to select or fit and half to score, and 20 independent replications of the whole experiment. Detection is by the top-$b$ rank of the coincidence statistic, and for every rule the ring picks the stock where its net benefit on the scoring episodes is largest. The rules are the uniform allocation, an allocation proportional to $N_w$, the rule of the proposition with the signal $S_w$ taken from Proposition~\ref{prop:power} (with the constant $\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{eff}}}=7.3$ fitted earlier in Section~\ref{sec:powercheck}, not on these data), the same rule with $S_w$ fitted per stock on the selection episodes, and an oracle that equalises the empirical net benefit on those episodes.

Figure~\ref{fig:alloc}a shows that the detection probability of the model (dotted lines) follows the simulated one, and that it is concave in $b$, which is what the proposition needs. The uniform allocation is expensive because the stocks differ so much: the ring in a stock of 2,500 accounts is caught with probability 0.2 only after 46 inspections, in a stock of 100 accounts after one (Figure~\ref{fig:alloc}a), so equal shares either leave the large stocks open or over-inspect the small ones. Table~\ref{tab:alloc} gives the capacity at which the ring has no positive net benefit in any stock: 384 per period for the uniform allocation at fine multiple 5 (median of 20 replications; range 256 to 512), 160 for the allocation proportional to the number of accounts, 128 for the rule with the signal from the law, and 160 for the oracle; at fine multiple 10 it is 176, 64, 64 and 64. At a budget below the deterrence level (C = 64) the ring's best net benefit is 37.5 thousand with the uniform allocation, 20.3 thousand with the proportional allocation and 13.2 thousand with the rule, a reduction of 65\% against uniform and of 35\% against proportional. The rule with the signal fitted on each stock does not do better than the proportional allocation (capacity 160), because 200 episodes per stock estimate the weak signal of large stocks badly, and the oracle, tuned on the same noisy episodes, is no better either (160). The rule is therefore worth using when the signal can be obtained from a law fitted elsewhere, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:power}, and not when it has to be estimated stock by stock.

Two limits apply. The experiment is synthetic: the stocks differ in $N_w$ only, with one ring signal and prizes assumed. In the agent-based market (stocks of 80 to 990 accounts, default ring with a low push probability) the ring is caught so easily (with probability at least 0.62 at five inspections and at least 0.24 at one) that a capacity of eight deters in every stock under every rule, so the rule has nothing to do there. And the proposition takes the allocation as a commitment that the ring can read; it also shows that randomising the allocation does not help, because the detection probability is concave.

\subsection{Other manipulation types and the cost to honest investors}

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} and Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} report, for the first calibrated market, what a fixed spoofer, pump-and-dump agent, wash pair and ring achieve under order-level rules and what the rules cost honest participants. In this market, minimum resting times and cancel fees do not reduce what the manipulators achieve: spoofing profit is 872 (s.e.\ 125) with no rule and 1,091 to 1,380 with minimum resting times or fees, and the spoofing price impact is higher, not lower, with a longer minimum resting time (0.26 against 0.11 ticks); pump impact is lower under cancel fees (0.31 against 0.47; 1.3 to 1.6 standard errors); wash volume is 3.1\% to 3.3\% of volume under every rule, and the ring's gain is unaffected except by the block price. In the uncalibrated default simulator (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}) a minimum resting time of 15 steps does reduce the profit of the same fixed spoofer, so the two simulators disagree on this question; we rely on the Vietnam preset because it matches the observed cancel share, tick and volatility, and the conclusion on order-level frictions holds for that preset only. These rules cost honest investors: a minimum resting time of 15 steps raises the spread by 7\% and volatility by 21\%, and a cancel fee of 2 raises volatility by 24\%. A tick twice as large widens the spread by 35\% and cuts depth by 10\% while lowering spoofing profit (491 against 872). The block price rule has no effect on honest trading in this experiment because honest agents do not trade blocks; its cost is the gap between the last price and the trailing mean, 0.17\% of price at 60 steps and 0.50\% at 240 steps in the first preset.

\subsection{Checking a lever against reality: the 2016 tick-size reform}\label{sec:tick}

The tick size is the one lever in the simulator for which a real natural experiment is available in public data. On 12 September 2016 the Ho Chi Minh Stock Exchange reduced its minimum tick (to 10, 50 and 100 VND below 10,000, between 10,000 and 49,950, and above 50,000 VND), (the tiers are from broker rule summaries, Appendix~\ref{app:calib}; \citet{vo2023} study this change and find lower trading costs), and we use HNX and UPCoM stocks as controls on the understanding that it applied to HOSE only. Using daily open, high, low, close and volume, we compare 282 HOSE stocks with 489 control stocks over the 40 trading days before and the 40 days from one week after the reform. Outcomes are the Corwin--Schultz high-low spread estimator, the daily range, the absolute return, the share of zero-return days and log volume. We could not establish the old tick from our sources (one summary gave 500 VND for all prices, which cannot be right given the small observed spread change), so we compare with simulated reductions of the tick by factors of 2, 5 and 10.

Table~\ref{tab:tickcompare} and Figure~\ref{fig:tick} show the result. The share of zero-return days fell by 7.1 points relative to controls (95\% interval $-9.2$ to $-5.0$), by 8.3 points with 20-day windows and by 6.7 with 60-day windows; the spread estimate fell by 0.06 points (8\% of its pre-reform level; interval $-0.13$ to $0.01$), and by 0.07 points with 60-day windows. Because one date and a bootstrap over stocks ignore common shocks, we also compute the same estimate at 57 placebo dates every ten trading days in 2015--2018 whose windows do not overlap the reform's (Figure~\ref{fig:tickplacebo}): the fall in the share of zero-return days is 4.3 standard deviations below the placebo mean and no placebo date is as extreme (placebo range $-3.1$ to $+4.1$ points), and a permutation of the exchange labels across stocks at the true date gives the same answer ($p<0.001$ in 5000 draws); the spread estimate, by contrast, is at the 12th percentile of the placebo distribution (two-sided placebo $p=0.26$, permutation $p=0.08$) and the daily range is not distinguishable from placebo variation at all. The weekly differences of the spread before the reform have a standard deviation of 0.08 points and no trend ($t=-0.8$), the post-reform mean difference is 0.8 of that standard deviation. Of the 389 HOSE stocks and 926 controls with data, 282 and 489 were listed before the start of the pre-reform window and enter the estimates. By price tercile the fall in the spread estimate is concentrated in low-priced stocks ($-0.18$ points, interval $-0.32$ to $-0.03$, against a pre-reform level of 1.0\%). The simulator predicts a fall of the share of zero-return days by 38\% for a halving of the tick, 43\% for a five-fold cut and 78\% for a ten-fold cut (real: 24\%), and of the spread estimate by 21\%, 65\% and 85\% (real: 8\%). The signs agree, in line with the finding of lower trading costs by \citet{vo2023} on intraday data; the real effect is of the order of the simulator's prediction for a halving of the tick and much smaller than for a five- or ten-fold cut. Prices in the panel are adjusted, so price bands are approximate, and the design cannot separate the reform from other changes on HOSE in the same weeks.
